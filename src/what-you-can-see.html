The game itself — the league’s rules, named as they happen

Icing

The puck is sent the length of the ice and play comes straight back. Watch where the faceoff goes — that dot is the whole punishment.

Faceoffs

Nine spots on the ice and the rule picks one. An icing brings the draw all the way back — same second as the whistle, and deep in the offending team’s own end.

Offside

This is the one rule we can draw but never replay: the feed records the call and the restart, never the crossing. So in a real game, watch the line rather than the play.

Diagram · then a real example

Penalties

A penalty is time off the ice: the offender sits, and his team plays a skater short — unless the other team commits a penalty of the same duration at the same time, and then both teams skate with the same number of players. The side with the extra skater is on a power play: the same penalty, named from the other bench.

Overtime

A hockey game does not end level. If the score is tied after sixty minutes, both teams drop to three skaters and a goaltender, and the next goal wins — five minutes of it, and a shootout after that if nobody scores. In the playoffs there is no shootout and nobody leaves the ice: they play full periods, five a side, until someone scores.

The empty net

Losing late, a team trades its goaltender for a sixth skater. Nothing is toggled here — the goalie is simply no longer on the ice.

Shots from the slot

A shot taken from inside the slot goes in 11.6% of the time — 19,618 goals from 168,850 attempts. From outside it, 3.3%. That gap is what the slot shading is for.

All situations

Hockey is not always five on five. A penalty puts one team a skater short — that team is killing it, the other is on the power play — and the count under the ice adds every situation together, which is what all situations means. Measured per 60 minutes in each: 95 attempts on the power play, 59 at even strength, 13 killing a penalty. So part of a team’s attempt lead can be nothing but the time it spent on the power play.

The attacking zone

Two blue lines cut the ice into three zones. The shaded strip at each one is a boundary one team is trying to hold play inside and the other to push it out. After a face-off in one team’s end, the team attacking that end takes 1.42 shot attempts before the next whistle, against 0.76 for the team defending it — across 169,633 draws. Those defending attempts are shots at the far end, which is what getting the puck out looks like; at the line itself we count nothing, because holding it leaves no event in the record.

How long a shift is

Nobody stays on the ice for long. Hockey is played in short bursts and skaters come off while the play is still running — a skater takes about 21 shifts a night, and a shift lasts a median of 46 seconds, 76% of them under a minute, measured over 3,144,105 shifts. So the five in front of you now are mostly not the five who were there a minute ago.

Score effects

A team that is behind takes more shot attempts, and a team that is ahead takes fewer — that is what score effects means. Measured in even-strength play: per 60 minutes of play with the same number of skaters on each side, a team takes 63 shot attempts while trailing, 59 while the score is level, and 53 while leading. So part of a team’s attempt lead can be nothing but the time it spent behind.
